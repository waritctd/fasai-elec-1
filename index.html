Block 0 · 0:00–0:40

Foundations: voltage, current, nodes

Goal: know what the symbols mean, be able to say which way current flows through a resistor, and find every node in a circuit. Nothing here is "the method" yet — but every mistake in nodal analysis traces back to one of these four ideas.

Tutor · 40 min

Don't lecture the whole 40 minutes. Spend ~10 min on current/voltage, ~8 on symbols, ~10 on Ohm's law with direction, ~12 on nodes (the colouring exercise). The one thing she must leave this block with: "voltage is a height; current flows downhill through a resistor, and the amount is the height difference divided by R." Say it five times today.

0.1 Current and voltage

Current (i, in amperes, A) is how much charge flows past a point per second — like litres per second in a pipe. It has a direction, which we draw as an arrow. If the true flow is opposite to our arrow, the number simply comes out negative. That is normal and not a mistake.

Voltage (v, in volts, V) is a difference in electrical height between two points. It's the "pressure" that pushes current. A voltage is always between two points: "the voltage across the resistor", "the voltage between node a and node b". When we say "the voltage at a node" we secretly mean "…relative to a point we've agreed to call 0 V" — the reference node, drawn with the ground symbol.

The picture to keep

Think of every node as a floor in a building. The reference node is the ground floor (0 V). A node voltage is just "which floor is this node on". A resistor is a slide between two floors: current slides down it, faster when the drop is bigger and slower when the slide is longer (bigger R). A voltage source is an elevator that fixes the floor difference between its two ends; a current source is a pump that forces a fixed flow no matter what.

0.2 The symbols you'll see on the quiz

  • Resistor — obeys Ohm's law, v = R·i. Always absorbs power.
  • Independent voltage source — fixes the voltage between its + and − ends (e.g. 12 V, + side is 12 V higher). The current through it is whatever the rest of the circuit needs — you cannot know it from the symbol.
  • Independent current source — forces exactly that current in the arrow direction. The voltage across it is whatever the circuit needs — it is not zero (the slides call this out as the classic beginner mistake).
  • Dependent sources (diamond) — same as above, but the value is gain × something else in the circuit: 3vx means "three times the voltage vx marked somewhere else". Every quiz has one. We'll handle them in Block 4.
  • Wire — zero resistance, so both ends are at exactly the same voltage. This is what makes a "node".
  • Ground — the node we declare to be 0 V. It's our choice; the answers (currents, voltage differences) don't depend on it.

0.3 Ohm's law — with direction

Rule 1 — the only formula you need for resistors

ia→b = (va − vb) / R

Pick a direction for the arrow. The current in that direction is (voltage where the arrow starts − voltage where it ends) ÷ R. Here: (8 − 2)/4 = 1.5 A flowing from a to b. If you had drawn the arrow the other way you'd get (2 − 8)/4 = −1.5 A — same physics, negative sign. Either is correct as long as the sign matches the arrow.

Node a is at 3 V, node b is at 11 V, and there's a 2 Ω resistor between them. What is the current ia→b (arrow pointing from a to b)?

0.4 Nodes — the skill everything depends on

A node is a whole set of points joined by wires, not a single dot. Because a wire has no resistance, every point on it is at the same voltage, so it all counts as one node. To find nodes: put your pen on a wire and follow it through every junction, stopping only when you hit a component. Everything you touched is one node.

0.5 Power — one sentence for now

Power absorbed by an element is p = v·i when the current arrow enters the + side of the voltage (this is the passive sign convention). Positive means it absorbs power (like every resistor); negative means it delivers power (like a battery doing its job). We'll come back to this in Block 5 because the quiz asks for it, but it's not needed to learn the method.

Block 1 · 0:40–1:05

Kirchhoff's current law & node voltages

Goal: write a KCL equation at any node using only node voltages, with the signs right every time. This one template is 80% of nodal analysis.

1.1 KCL: what goes in must come out

At every node, the sum of currents leaving = the sum of currents entering. Equivalently, if we call "leaving" positive and "entering" negative, the sum of all currents at a node is zero. In the figure: 3 A + 5 A enter, 2 A leaves, so the unknown must carry 6 A out.

1.2 Putting Rule 1 and KCL together

Now the trick that makes nodal analysis work. Stand on a node whose voltage is vn. Every resistor attached to it leads to some other node vm. By Rule 1, the current leaving through that resistor is (vn − vm)/R — always "me minus neighbour". You never need to guess a direction: you always point the arrow away from the node you're standing on.

Rule 2 — the KCL template (write this at every unknown node)

at node n Σ (vn − vneighbour)/R  +  Σ (current-source terms) = 0

Resistor to a neighbour: (vn − vm)/R.   Resistor to ground: vn/R (because ground is 0).   Current source with the arrow pointing into the node: write − I.   Arrow pointing out of the node: write + I.

Some people prefer "resistor currents leaving = source currents entering", i.e. move the sources to the right-hand side with the opposite sign. Same equation. Pick one form and use it all day.

Tutor · say it this way

"Stand on the node. Look down each wire. Through a resistor, current leaves you at (me − them)/R. A current source pointing at you is bringing current in, so on the 'leaving' side it's negative." Have her say "me minus them" out loud while writing each term for the rest of the day. It kills 90% of sign errors.

Block 2 · 1:15–2:00

The nodal recipe

Goal: run the full five-step method on circuits with resistors and current sources, including a 3-node circuit that needs three simultaneous equations.

2.1 Why we need a method

Series/parallel simplification and voltage/current division only work on ladder-shaped circuits. The moment a circuit has a bridge, a source in the middle, or a dependent source, they fail. Nodal analysis is a systematic method: it always works, it always produces exactly as many equations as unknowns, and — the important part for a quiz — it always looks the same, so you can do it under time pressure.

The recipe (from the course slides, in the order you'll actually do it)

  1. Find the nodes. Choose a reference node (ground, 0 V). Best choice: the node touching the most elements, or the − end of a voltage source.
  2. Label every other node v1, v2, … If a voltage source connects a node straight to ground, that node's voltage is already known — write the number, not a symbol.
  3. Write KCL at every unknown node using the Rule 2 template ("me minus them over R", sources ± I).
  4. Clear the fractions and put in standard form: multiply each equation by the smallest number that kills all denominators, collect the v's on the left, numbers on the right.
  5. Solve the simultaneous equations (substitution, elimination, or the equation mode on your calculator). Then answer the actual question: the current or voltage asked for, using Rule 1. Finally plug back into one KCL to check.

Worked example A — two nodes, current sources only

original · same shape as slide example 2

  1. Nodes and reference

    Three nodes: the top-left wire (v1), the top-right wire (v2), and the entire bottom wire. The bottom wire touches every element, so it's the natural ground. Two unknowns → we need two equations.

  2. KCL at v1 — stand on the node, look down each wire

    4 Ω down to ground: v1/4.  2 Ω across to v2: (v1 − v2)/2.  3 A source pointing into the node: −3.

    node 1 v1/4 + (v1 − v2)/2 − 3 = 0

  3. KCL at v2

    2 Ω back to v1: (v2 − v1)/2 — notice it's "me minus them" again, so the order flips compared with the previous equation. 4 Ω to ground: v2/4. 1 A source into the node: −1.

    node 2 (v2 − v1)/2 + v2/4 − 1 = 0

  4. Standard form (multiply both by 4)

    (1) 3v1 − 2v2 = 12
    (2) −2v1 + 3v2 = 4

    Sanity check that you'll see on every well-formed nodal system: the coefficient of the node's own voltage is positive and biggest; the neighbours' coefficients are negative. If a sign breaks that pattern, look for an error.

  5. Solve

    from (1): v1 = (12 + 2v2)/3
    into (2): −2(12 + 2v2)/3 + 3v2 = 4  →  −24 − 4v2 + 9v2 = 12  →  5v2 = 36
    v2 = 7.2 V,  v1 = 8.8 V

  6. Answer the question & check

    Current in the 2 Ω resistor, left to right: (8.8 − 7.2)/2 = 0.8 A. Check KCL at node 1: 8.8/4 + 0.8 = 2.2 + 0.8 = 3 A — exactly the source current. ✓

Tutor · how to run a worked example

Reveal one step at a time and, before each reveal, ask her to predict it on paper: "Write KCL at node 1 before I show it." Compare, then reveal. Step 4's pattern (own coefficient positive and largest, neighbours negative) is worth 2 minutes — it's her fastest error detector on the quiz.

Block 3 · 2:00–2:40

Voltage sources & supernodes

Goal: recognise the two cases for a voltage source in nodal analysis — grounded (a node you already know) and floating (a supernode) — and handle both without hesitation.

Voltage sources are awkward for KCL for one reason: you don't know the current through them (Block 0). So you can't write a "current leaving through the source" term. There are exactly two situations, and each has a fix.

3.1 Case A — one end of the source is at ground: the node is known

If a voltage source sits between ground and node a, then va is simply the source value (with its sign: + side up means va = +12 V). You don't write KCL at that node at all — you have one fewer unknown. The current through the source, if the question asks, comes at the very end from KCL at that node.

Worked example B — grounded source

original

  1. Nodes

    Ground = bottom wire. Node a is fixed by the source: va = 12 V. Only vb is unknown. One equation.

  2. KCL at b

    node b (vb − 12)/3 + vb/6 − 1 = 0
    ×6 2vb − 24 + vb = 6  →  vb = 10 V

    The known node appears in the equation as a number, "me minus 12", never as a symbol.

  3. Anything else asked, then check

    Current through the 3 Ω from a to b: (12 − 10)/3 = 0.667 A. Current out of the 12 V source = the same 0.667 A (it's the only thing attached to a besides ground). Check at b: 0.667 in from the resistor + 1 in from the source = 1.667 A out through 6 Ω = 10/6 = 1.667 ✓.

Your turn Slide example 9 — find ib

Node a is known (25 V). Write KCL at b and c, solve, then ib = (vb − vc)/20. Slide answer: ib = −0.259 A. Before you start: what does the negative sign in the answer mean?

Check the equations
node b (vb − 25)/10 + vb/10 + (vb − vc)/20 = 0  ×20 → 5vb − vc = 50
node c (vc − 25)/5 + (vc − vb)/20 + vc/20 = 0  ×20 → −vb + 6vc = 100

Don't forget the 5 Ω on top — it joins a and c, so it belongs to node c's equation (as "me minus 25 over 5").

Check the answer
vb = 13.79 V,  vc = 18.97 V
ib = (13.79 − 18.97)/20 = −0.259 A

Negative: the arrow was drawn b → c, but c is higher than b, so 0.259 A actually flows c → b. Write the answer as −0.259 A with the arrow as drawn — that's what the marker wants.

3.2 Case B — the source floats between two unknown nodes: supernode

Now neither end is ground. The source's current is unknown, so KCL at either end alone contains an unknown we can't express. The fix from slide 52: draw a dashed bubble around both nodes and the source and treat the bubble as one big node.

Rule 3 — supernode = one KCL + one KVL

KCL for the bubble: add up the currents leaving the bubble through everything attached to either node, exactly as in Rule 2, but skip the source inside the bubble (its current enters one node and leaves the other — it cancels).

KVL inside the bubble: v+ side − v− side = Vsource. That's the equation you "lost".

Count: two unknown nodes, one KCL (bubble) + one KVL (constraint) = two equations. Always balances.

Block 4 · 2:50–3:30

Dependent sources

Goal: handle the diamond. Every quiz nodal question has one. The method does not change — there is exactly one extra line to write.

Rule 4 — a dependent source is an ordinary source plus one translation line

  1. Write the KCL (or supernode KCL + KVL) exactly as if the source were independent, using its label (e.g. 2ix) as if it were a number.
  2. Write one extra line that expresses the controlling variable in node voltages: a controlling current through a resistor is Rule 1, ix = (vtail − vhead)/R; a controlling voltage is a difference of node voltages, vx = v+ − v−.
  3. Substitute, collect, solve. Same number of equations as before.

The slides (57–60) do precisely this: "write KCL including the current of the controlled source just as if it were an ordinary current source; next find an expression for the controlling variable in terms of the node voltages; substitute."

Block 5 · 3:30–3:55

Quiz technique & timed drill

Goal: get the power question right (where marks were lost on the last quiz), know exactly what to write on the page, and do one full problem against the clock.

5.1 Power of a source — the sign is the whole question

After nodal analysis you know every node voltage, so you know the voltage across every source. The quiz then asks "the power of the independent source". Two clean rules, both from the passive sign convention:

Rule 5 — power delivered by a source

current source pdelivered = I × (vhead − vtail)  voltage at the arrowhead minus voltage at the tail
voltage source pdelivered = V × (current flowing out of the + terminal)

Positive → the source is delivering (generating) power. Negative → it is absorbing power, which is allowed and happens on quizzes whenever a dependent source is doing the pushing. If the question says "power absorbed", flip the sign. Always state which one you're giving: "the 2 A source absorbs 24 W".

Two examples you already have the numbers for:

  • Your-turn circuit in Block 4 (2 A source, arrow up into v2 = −12 V): pdel = 2 × (−12 − 0) = −24 W. The 2 A source absorbs 24 W — the dependent source is supplying the circuit. Energy check: resistors absorb 12²/30 + 48²/20 = 4.8 + 115.2 = 120 W; dependent source delivers 60 V × 2.4 A = 144 W = 120 + 24 ✓.
  • Worked example B (12 V source, 0.667 A leaves its + terminal): pdel = 12 × 0.667 = 8 W delivered.

The fix is mechanical: write the node voltages at the arrowhead and at the tail on the diagram, then apply Rule 5. Never guess the sign from intuition.

5.2 What a full-mark answer looks like

  1. On the diagram: mark ground, label every unknown node, draw the supernode bubble if any, and mark any current you define with an arrow.
  2. Equations, labelled: "KCL at v1: …", "Supernode: …", "Constraint: …", "ix = …". The marker gives method marks for these even if the arithmetic slips.
  3. Standard form with the fractions cleared, then the solution as decimals (quiz rule). Keep 3–4 significant figures until the end.
  4. The thing that was asked, in a box, with units and — for power — the word "delivers" or "absorbs".
  5. One-line check: plug into one KCL. Write it down; it's evidence you know what you're doing, and it catches most errors.

5.3 The mistakes that cost marks

  • ×
    Counting dots as nodes.

    A node is everything joined by wire. Trace it. (Block 0)

  • ×
    "Them minus me".

    Every resistor term is (this node − neighbour)/R. Say it out loud. (Rule 2)

  • ×
    Sign of a current source.

    Arrow into the node → −I in the "leaving = 0" form. Arrow out → +I.

  • ×
    Writing a current for a voltage source.

    You don't know it. Grounded → known node. Floating → supernode + constraint. (Rule 3)

  • ×
    Forgetting the translation line.

    A dependent source always needs ix or vx written in node voltages. (Rule 4)

  • ×
    Assuming the voltage across a current source is zero.

    It's whatever the nodes say it is. The slides explicitly warn about this.

  • ×
    Power sign by feel.

    Use Rule 5 with the node voltages at the arrowhead/tail (or the current out of +).

  • ×
    Rounding too early / leaving fractions.

    Keep 4 s.f. through the algebra, write decimals at the end.

Challenge — Exercise sheet #2, problem 1

two supernodes

Use nodal analysis to find v4. (Sheet answer: 63.06 V.) Take the bottom node as ground. Sources: the 100 V source has its + toward T; the 150 V battery has its + (long plate) toward R; the 5 A source pushes from C up to T; the 10 A source pushes from the bottom node toward R.

Hint

Two floating voltage sources → two supernodes: {T, L} and {C, R}. That gives two bubble KCLs plus two constraints for four unknowns. v4 is just vC (the 25 Ω goes to ground). Diagonal resistors are still just resistors: "me minus them over R".

Equations
constraints vT − vL = 100;   vR − vC = 150
supernode {T,L} (vT − vR)/12.5 − 5 + (vL − vC)/20 + vL/10 = 0
supernode {C,R} vC/25 + (vC − vL)/20 + 5 + (vR − vT)/12.5 − 10 = 0

The 5 A source leaves C (+5) and enters T (−5); it shows up in both bubbles with opposite signs because the bubbles are different. The 10 A enters R → −10.

Answer
vT = 174.8 V · vL = 74.77 V · vR = 213.1 V · vC = v4 = 63.06 V
